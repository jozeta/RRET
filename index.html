Vector Optics Reference

1. What is a Milliradian (MIL / MRAD)?

A Milliradian (MRAD) is an angular measurement equal to 1/1000th of a radian. Because it is a 1:1000 proportional angle, 1 MIL subtends exactly 1 unit of measurement at a distance of 1000 units.

  • 1 MIL at 100 meters = 10 cm (0.1 meters)
  • 1 MIL at 500 meters = 50 cm (0.5 meters)
  • 1 MIL at 1000 meters = 1.0 meter (100 cm)
  • 1 MIL at 100 yards = 3.6 inches
  • 1 MIL at 1000 yards = 36 inches (1 yard)

2. First Focal Plane (FFP) vs SFP Scopes

In a First Focal Plane (FFP) scope, the reticle is positioned in front of the optical zoom assembly. As magnification changes, the reticle scales up or down in exact sync with the target image.

Why FFP is Superior for Range Estimation: MIL subtensions remain 100% accurate at ALL magnification levels (e.g. 3x to 25x). In Second Focal Plane (SFP) scopes, reticle subtensions are only accurate at one specific calibration zoom (usually max zoom).

3. Vector Optics 4-Step Measuring Protocol

STEP 1
Identify Known Size

Determine known target dimension. E.g., Deer chest depth (~45 cm / 0.45m), Swedish B65 target (65 cm / 0.65m), or 30 cm Gong.

STEP 2
Bracket the Target

Align top or bottom edge of target exactly on a primary reticle line or hash mark.

STEP 3
Read MIL Subtension

Count MIL hashes down to opposite edge. Use fine 0.2 MIL or 0.5 MIL tick marks to measure down to 0.1 MIL precision.

STEP 4
Apply Distance Formula

Divide (Target Size × 1000) by measured MIL subtension to get exact range in meters or yards.

8. Common Mistakes in MIL Range Estimation

Reading SFP Subtensions at the Wrong Zoom

On a Second Focal Plane scope, MIL hashes are only true to scale at one calibrated magnification (usually max power). Reading them at any other zoom silently produces a wrong distance — this is exactly why this simulator uses First Focal Plane.

Canting the Reticle

Tilting the rifle rotates the MIL grid off the target's true vertical/horizontal axis. Even a slight cant makes the bracketed subtension too large, so the calculated range comes out short.

Bracketing the Wrong Edge

Measure the exact same feature the "known size" reference describes — outer edge to outer edge. Splitting the difference toward the center, or including/excluding a rim or base the reference doesn't count, throws the math off before it even starts.

Ignoring Slope on Angled Shots

Distance estimation gives the line-of-sight (slant) range. On steep uphill/downhill shots, gravity only acts on the horizontal component — feed the slant range into a drop calculator uncorrected and you'll overestimate holdover. Apply Horizontal Range = Slant Range × cos(θ) first (see section 5).

Mixing Unit Systems

MOA and MIL are different angular units (1 MOA ≈ 0.291 MIL) with different formulas and constants (27.77 for MIL/yards, 95.5 for MOA/yards). Confirm which reticle and which formula you're using before doing the math.

Trusting a Stale "Known Size"

Reference sizes assume a specific target variant (e.g. an adult chest depth, or a specific competition target's official dimensions). Using the wrong reference — an average when you're looking at an outlier, or last year's target spec — carries its error straight through to the final range.

About RRET

Stadiametric range estimation — figuring out how far away something is by comparing its known real-world size to how large it appears through a MIL/MRAD reticle — is a core field skill, but it's genuinely hard to practice safely and often. You need a scope, a rangefinder to check your work, and a series of targets of verified size at unknown distances. Most shooters only get to drill it a handful of times a season, usually right before a match or a hunt.

RRET turns that drill into something you can run whenever you want. It puts a true First Focal Plane reticle in front of a target of known dimensions at a distance it doesn't tell you, and asks you to work out the range the same way you would with a real rifle scope: bracket the target in MILs, apply the formula, enter your answer, see exactly how far off you were and why.

Who It's For

  • Swedish field shooting (fältskytte) competitors practicing against the official Gevär 6,5 and Korthåll (.22LR) target specifications used in sanctioned matches.
  • PRS and IPSC shooters drilling range calls against steel poppers and metric silhouettes before a match.
  • Hunters building the instinct to size up game and terrain features at a glance, without a rangefinder in hand.
  • Anyone learning MIL/MRAD optics from scratch — the Learn hub walks through the underlying math step by step, not just the simulator.

How the FFP Engine Works

Every target is defined by a real height and width in meters. At scenario start, RRET picks a random (or filtered) target and distance, then computes exactly how many milliradians that target's edges subtend at that range. The reticle and the target are rendered from the same MIL-to-pixel scale, so — like a real First Focal Plane scope, and unlike a Second Focal Plane one — the reticle's hash marks stay true to their stated MIL value at every magnification from 1x to 25x. Bracket the target with the reticle, read the subtension, and the math is identical to what you'd do on a real rifle in the field.

Scoring compares your answer to the actual distance by percentage error, and every round is logged to your local training history so you can track whether you're actually getting better over time.
